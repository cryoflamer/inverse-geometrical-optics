\section{Comparison with a Spherical Surface}
\label{sec:spherical-comparison}

The exponential refractive profile and a spherical surface produce the same
leading square-root dependence of horizon distance on endpoint height, but
they are not identical when the same numerical height is used in both models.
We now compare the two exact laws and quantify their difference.

\subsection{Exact spherical horizon law}

Consider a sphere of radius $R$ and a point at radial height $h$ above its
surface. Let $\alpha$ be the central angle between the point's radial line and
the radius through the tangency point of the limiting line of sight. The
corresponding right triangle gives
\begin{equation}
    \cos\alpha
    =
    \frac{R}{R+h}.
    \label{eq:spherical-horizon-angle}
\end{equation}
The surface distance from the tangency point to the foot of the elevated point
is therefore
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{s}}(h)
    =
    R\alpha
    =
    R\arccos\!\left(\frac{R}{R+h}\right).
    \label{eq:exact-spherical-horizon-distance}
\end{equation}
For observer and target heights $h_{\mathrm{o}}$ and $h_{\mathrm{t}}$, the
maximum surface separation before mutual occlusion is
\begin{equation}
    L_{\max}^{(\mathrm{s})}
    =
    d_{\mathrm{s}}(h_{\mathrm{o}})
    +
    d_{\mathrm{s}}(h_{\mathrm{t}}).
    \label{eq:exact-spherical-maximum-separation}
\end{equation}
Here the comparison variable is surface arc length. This is the quantity
corresponding to the horizontal coordinate $x=R\phi$ in the conformal map of
Sec.~\ref{sec:conformal-equivalence}. The straight-line distance from the
elevated point to the tangency point,
\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{h(2R+h)},
\end{equation}
is a different geometric quantity and is not used in the comparison below.

\subsection{Exact refractive and spherical laws}

For the Earth-scale exponential profile, Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-exponential-horizon-distance}
with $\kappa=1/R$ gives
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{e}}(h)
    =
    R\arccos\!\left(e^{-h/R}\right).
    \label{eq:earth-scale-exponential-distance}
\end{equation}
The two exact horizon laws are thus
\begin{align}
    d_{\mathrm{e}}(h)
    &=
    R\arccos\!\left(e^{-h/R}\right),
    \\
    d_{\mathrm{s}}(h)
    &=
    R\arccos\!\left(\frac{1}{1+h/R}\right).
    \label{eq:refractive-spherical-pair}
\end{align}
Their difference arises entirely from the relation between physical height and
transformed radial height. Under Eq.~\eqref{eq:conformal-coordinate-map},
a flat-model height $h$ corresponds to the circular radius
\begin{equation}
    r=Re^{h/R}.
\end{equation}
Consequently, the exponential model is exactly equivalent to a circular model
whose radial height is
\begin{equation}
    H(h)
    =
    R\left(e^{h/R}-1\right),
    \label{eq:equivalent-radial-height}
\end{equation}
not to a circular model whose radial height is numerically equal to $h$. In
fact,
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{e}}(h)
    =
    d_{\mathrm{s}}\!\left(H(h)\right)
    \label{eq:exact-height-reparameterization}
\end{equation}
holds exactly. The mismatch at equal heights is therefore due to coordinate
reparameterization, not a failure of the conformal equivalence.

\subsection{Small-height comparison}

Let
\begin{equation}
    z=\frac{h}{R}.
\end{equation}
For $z\ll 1$, the refractive distance has the expansion already obtained in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-distance-expansion},
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{e}}(h)
    =
    \sqrt{2Rh}
    \left[
        1-\frac{z}{6}+\frac{z^2}{120}
        +O(z^3)
    \right].
    \label{eq:refractive-distance-small-height}
\end{equation}
The spherical arc distance expands as
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{s}}(h)
    =
    \sqrt{2Rh}
    \left[
        1-\frac{5z}{12}+\frac{43z^2}{160}
        +O(z^3)
    \right].
    \label{eq:spherical-distance-small-height}
\end{equation}
Both laws therefore reduce to
\begin{equation}
    d(h)\simeq\sqrt{2Rh}
\end{equation}
at leading order. Their first difference appears one order later:
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{e}}(h)-d_{\mathrm{s}}(h)
    =
    \sqrt{2Rh}
    \left[
        \frac{z}{4}
        -\frac{25z^2}{96}
        +O(z^3)
    \right].
    \label{eq:refractive-spherical-difference}
\end{equation}
Hence the relative discrepancy is of order $h/R$. For ordinary terrestrial
heights this is extremely small even though the two laws are not exactly the
same at equal numerical height.

\subsection{Numerical scale of the discrepancy}

Table~\ref{tab:horizon-law-comparison} illustrates the difference for
$R=6371\,\mathrm{km}$. The listed distances are one-sided horizon distances
from a tangency point to an endpoint at height $h$.

\begin{table}[ht]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{rccc}
        \hline
        $h$ & $d_{\mathrm{e}}$ (km) & $d_{\mathrm{s}}$ (km)
            & $d_{\mathrm{e}}-d_{\mathrm{s}}$ \\
        \hline
        $2\,\mathrm{m}$   & 5.048168 & 5.048167 & $0.40\,\mathrm{mm}$ \\
        $10\,\mathrm{m}$  & 11.288044 & 11.288039 & $4.43\,\mathrm{mm}$ \\
        $100\,\mathrm{m}$ & 35.695845 & 35.695705 & $0.140\,\mathrm{m}$ \\
        $1\,\mathrm{km}$  & 112.877515 & 112.873086 & $4.43\,\mathrm{m}$ \\
        $10\,\mathrm{km}$ & 356.866007 & 356.726164 & $139.84\,\mathrm{m}$ \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Exact exponential-refractive and spherical horizon distances at
    equal numerical height for $R=6371\,\mathrm{km}$.}
    \label{tab:horizon-law-comparison}
\end{table}

At low altitude, the numerical agreement is substantially stronger than
equality of the leading square-root term alone would suggest. Nevertheless,
with sufficiently large heights or sufficiently precise measurements the two
models are distinguishable. Exact agreement at equal physical height would
require a different refractive-index profile, which can be reconstructed from
the desired visibility law by the inverse method developed later in the
paper.
