\section{Comparison with the Physical Atmosphere}
\label{sec:physical-atmosphere}

The inverse construction of Sec.~\ref{sec:inverse-reconstruction} establishes
that a stratified refractive medium can be designed mathematically to reproduce
a prescribed horizon law above a flat opaque boundary.  The remaining question
is whether the required profile resembles the refractive-index structure of an
ordinary atmosphere.  The decisive distinction is the sign of the vertical
refractive-index gradient.

\subsection{Gradient required by the optical construction}

The Earth-scale exponential model is
\begin{equation}
    n_{\mathrm{e}}(h)=n_0 e^{h/R},
    \label{eq:required-exponential-profile}
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dn_{\mathrm{e}}}{dh}
    =
    \frac{n_{\mathrm{e}}(h)}{R}
    >0.
    \label{eq:required-positive-gradient}
\end{equation}
The exact profile reconstructed from the spherical horizon law,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact-spherical-reconstruction-profile}, has the same qualitative
behaviour.  Writing
\begin{equation}
    \frac{n_{\mathrm{s}}^2(h)}{n_0^2}
    =
    1+
    \frac{(R+h)^2h(2R+h)}{R^4},
\end{equation}
the polynomial multiplying $R^{-4}$ is strictly increasing for $h>0$.
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dn_{\mathrm{s}}}{dh}>0
    \qquad (h>0).
    \label{eq:reconstructed-positive-gradient}
\end{equation}
Thus both the asymptotically matched exponential profile and the exact inverse
profile require the refractive index to increase with altitude.

This sign is also consistent with the ray geometry derived earlier.  In a
stratified isotropic medium, rays bend toward larger refractive index.  A
profile with $dn/dh>0$ therefore bends rays upward, allowing a ray connecting
two elevated endpoints to have a lower turning point and to graze the opaque
boundary between them.  That lower turning point is the optical analogue of a
geometric tangency point on a convex surface.

\subsection{Ordinary atmospheric stratification}

Away from strong local inversions, the refractive index of air decreases with
height because density and pressure decrease upward.  A simple idealised
profile is
\begin{equation}
    n_{\mathrm{a}}(h)
    \simeq
    1+(n_0-1)e^{-h/H},
    \label{eq:ordinary-atmosphere-profile}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is an atmospheric scale height.  Its derivative is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dn_{\mathrm{a}}}{dh}
    =
    -\frac{n_0-1}{H}e^{-h/H}
    <0.
    \label{eq:ordinary-negative-gradient}
\end{equation}
The ordinary atmosphere therefore has the opposite gradient sign from
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:required-positive-gradient} and
\eqref{eq:reconstructed-positive-gradient}.  Its rays bend toward the denser,
higher-index air below rather than upward toward increasing index.

The mismatch is not merely quantitative.  Altering the scale height or the
small surface refractivity $n_0-1$ changes the magnitude of the gradient but
not its sign.  A monotonically decreasing profile cannot be continuously tuned
into the required increasing profile without passing through a qualitatively
different stratification.

\subsection{Scale of the required variation}

For the exponential construction, the fractional gradient is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{n}\frac{dn}{dh}
    =
    \frac{1}{R}.
    \label{eq:required-fractional-gradient}
\end{equation}
Near the boundary this gives
\begin{equation}
    n(h)-n_0
    \simeq
    n_0\frac{h}{R}.
    \label{eq:required-near-surface-index-change}
\end{equation}
The exact inverse profile has the same linear term, as shown in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:spherical-profile-small-height}.  Hence the leading requirement
is not an arbitrarily chosen local anomaly: it is fixed by the radius $R$ of
the spherical horizon law being reproduced.

The absolute change over terrestrial heights is small because $h/R\ll1$.
Smallness, however, does not remove the sign conflict.  A weak positive
gradient and a weak negative gradient bend rays in opposite directions and
therefore generate different turning-point structures.

\subsection{Exceptional atmospheric conditions}

Real atmospheres need not be perfectly monotonic.  Temperature inversions and
sharp boundary layers can produce anomalous refraction, ducting, mirages, and
multiple ray paths.  Such effects can substantially shift an apparent horizon
or distort individual objects.  They do not, however, make the ordinary
atmosphere globally equivalent to the smooth profile reconstructed above.
Exact reproduction of the spherical visibility law for arbitrary endpoint
heights requires the prescribed positive profile throughout the relevant
height interval, not merely a local layer that occasionally bends selected
rays in the required direction.

Accordingly, two claims must be kept separate.  First, inverse geometrical
optics shows that a mathematical refractive profile above a flat boundary can
reproduce the same limiting horizon law as a sphere.  Second, the profile
required to do so is not the refractive-index profile of an ordinary
atmosphere: its vertical gradient has the opposite sign.  The construction is
therefore a useful optical equivalence, but not a model of standard atmospheric
refraction.
